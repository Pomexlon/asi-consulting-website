HORIZON 01 · THE COLLATZ CONJECTUREESTABLISHED OPEN PROBLEM

Two simple rules.
An infinite question.

Must every positive whole number eventually reach 1?

Start with a positive whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, multiply it by three and add one. Repeat. The conjecture says every starting number eventually reaches 1. [1, 2]

WHAT WOULD SETTLE IT?

Every case.
Not just many cases.

A proof must cover every positive starting integer. A disproof must rigorously establish an exception—for example, a nontrivial cycle or a trajectory that never reaches 1.

A large number or a run that reaches this calculator’s limit is not such a disproof.

OUR PROPOSED FIRST STEP

Find a statement
small enough to check.

Study a precisely defined family of trajectories. Search for counterexamples to a proposed rule, then attempt a proof of whatever survives. Compare it with existing work before describing it as new.

Current INAI status: this brief and the explorer are prepared. No new Collatz result or general proof is claimed.

A small, checkable foothold—not a breakthrough

For a starting value n = 4k + 1 > 1, three standard Collatz steps give:

4k + 1 → 12k + 4 → 6k + 2 → 3k + 1

Since k > 0, the last value is smaller than the start. For example: 13 → 40 → 20 → 10.

This elementary reduction does not prove the whole conjecture: it does not establish that every other starting number eventually gets smaller, or that every smaller value reaches 1. It is included as a worked explanation, not a claim of original mathematics.

RESEARCH CONTEXT

An established problem. An honest starting point.

Collatz is listed as open in Thomas Bloom’s Erdős Problems database at our source check on 18 October 2026. Tao’s published result concerns “almost all” trajectories in a precise density sense; it is not a proof that every starting number reaches 1. [1, 2]

The database uses an accelerated convention that combines an odd step with its first halving. The calculator above uses separate standard steps; the reach-1 question is equivalent, but step counts differ.

HORIZON 02 · BEYOND THE LAST HUMAN EXPERTINAI-SET THOUGHT EXPERIMENT

The AI makes the discovery.
It designs the test.
It writes the checker.

Have we verified a discovery—or trusted the same intelligence three times?

Imagine a future system proposes a scientific result that no human team can independently derive. It also supplies an explanation, a test and the software declaring that the test passed.

Humans may still understand the claim, the measurement protocol and limited checks. The difficulty is that the complete derivation lies beyond their practical reach. What evidence would justify accepting the result without simply trusting its author?

THE CHALLENGE

Can the sophistication of the proposer grow
without silently expanding what the human must trust?

THE IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Unable to discover
is not unable to check.

Finding an answer and checking a supplied answer can be very different tasks. You need not have discovered the factors of 10,403 to check that 101 × 103 = 10,403.

This is a simple illustration of the distinction—not a solution to verification of arbitrary scientific claims.

THE LIMITING CASE

Where does the
trust chain end?

If the evaluator receives only the proposer’s self-reports, an accurate system and a mistaken one could produce the same report. That report alone cannot distinguish the two.

The useful challenge is to specify what additional check, observation or explicit assumption breaks that ambiguity—and what it still cannot establish.

A BOUNDED RESEARCH BRIEF

Make the unknown
small enough to evaluate.

Choose a task family with independently established answers. Give a strong proposer a larger search budget than its evaluator. Include correct outputs and deliberately faulty supporting evidence.

Compare explanation alone, model critique and an explicit external checking protocol. Declare the budgets, the checks humans can understand and the assumptions retained.

WHAT WE WOULD MEASURE

Neither believe everything.
Nor reject everything.

Measure acceptance of correct claims, mistaken acceptance of false claims, unresolved cases, human effort and total cost. Use repeated, held-out trials.

A useful protocol must reduce false acceptance while retaining the ability to accept correct results. Performance on one task family would not establish general verification of superhuman intelligence.
