FIELD NOTE 00118 OCTOBER 2026INAI-SET CHALLENGEPROPOSED APPROACH

An AI can remember the correction—
and still act on the mistake.

When information changes, which decisions should an AI stop trusting?

Evaluation not yet run.

THE QUESTION

A changed fact.
An unchanged plan.

“Imagine an AI helps me organise a project. A supplier changes the delivery date. The AI acknowledges the new date, but the installation plan still depends on the old one.

How could we make a correction change the decisions that depend on it—not just the next answer?”
◇
An INAI-created project scenario

Fictional example · Question set by INAI.

HOPE’S PROPOSED APPROACH

Remember what
the decision depends on.

We would start with a different question. Not only “What is the latest delivery date?”, but “Which decisions depend on that date?”

Give each consequential decision a short record of the facts and permissions supporting it. When one changes, flag the dependent decisions for rechecking.

01Record the supporting facts and permissions.
02Trace which decisions depend on a change.
03Review affected work. Preserve what remains valid.

Track the supporting facts, permissions and decisions.

THE HYPOTHESIS

Correction should travel
with its consequences.

For tasks involving changing facts, explicitly tracking what each decision depends on may reduce stale actions compared with a workflow that merely adds corrections to its conversation history.

HOW WE WOULD TEST IT

Matched tasks.
Repeated trials.

Use the same underlying model, project tasks, tools and resource limits, with and without the proposed tracking method. Change a relevant fact or permission during each task.

Measure correct updates, preserved valid work, completed tasks, human intervention, time and cost. Report failures as well as successes.

A system that avoids mistakes only by refusing to finish anything would not meet our practical goal.

RESEARCH CONTEXT

A long-standing idea. A modern question.

The idea builds on truth-maintenance research: recording what supports a belief and revisiting its consequences when that support changes. Jon Doyle’s A Truth Maintenance System (1979) is part of that history.

Our proposed contribution is to test an implementation in a modern AI-assisted workflow, including its effect on completion, time and complexity.
